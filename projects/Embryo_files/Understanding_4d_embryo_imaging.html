Can a computer reconstruct the developmental history of an organism simply by observing cells under a microscope? That question became the starting point of this research journal.

Rather than immediately applying machine learning models, this work documents the process of understanding the biological problem itself. The first phase focuses on developmental biology, fluorescence microscopy, multidimensional imaging, and the computational challenges associated with interpreting four-dimensional microscopy datasets.

Although this document is written from the perspective of someone exploring computational biology from an engineering background, every concept is intentionally explained so that readers without prior biological knowledge can follow along. The objective is not merely to build an AI model, but to understand why such models are needed in the first place.

Why This Research ?

Most computer vision problems begin with an image. This one begins with life itself. Imagine recording every division of a single fertilized cell until it eventually becomes an entire organism. Every daughter cell. Every movement. Every decision. Every developmental branch. Now imagine asking a computer to reconstruct that entire story automatically. That is the scientific question that motivated this exploration.

At first glance, it sounds like a standard object detection problem. It isn't. The more I read about developmental biology, the more I realized the objective is not simply detecting cells. The objective is understanding their history. That distinction completely changes both the biology and the AI.

My Initial (Incorrect) Assumption

I initially assumed biological imaging worked similarly to conventional computer vision. I expected something resembling the pipeline like :

Microscope -> Image -> Machine Learning -> Predictions

This assumption survived approximately five minutes. The first microscopy dataset I explored did not contain ordinary images. Instead, it introduced me to multidimensional scientific data structures, unfamiliar storage formats, and the realization that biological experiments rarely fit inside traditional image representations. Nature, it turns out, has little interest in JPEGs.

The Dataset That Refused To Behave Like A Photograph

One of the earliest surprises was discovering that microscopy experiments are frequently stored using formats designed for large multidimensional datasets rather than conventional image files. Initially, this felt unnecessarily complicated. After understanding the scale of the data, it became obvious why. One embryo contained data organized approximately as (Time, Depth, Height, Width) rather than (Height, Width) The dataset was therefore not an image. Nor was it simply a video. It represented a sequence of three-dimensional observations acquired over developmental time. In hindsight, describing it as a four-dimensional movie feels much more accurate than calling it an image dataset.

The Four Numbers That Explained Everything

One of the first commands executed during exploration returned

image.shape

(100, 64, 256, 256)

At first glance these appeared to be arbitrary dimensions. They later became one of the most informative observations made during this phase of the project.

100 Time points 64 Optical slices 256 Height 256 Width

This single output completely changed my mental model. Instead of analyzing one photograph, I was examining one hundred developmental time points, each consisting of sixty-four optical sections through the embryo. The implications for computer vision immediately became obvious. Every cell exists simultaneously in space and time.
