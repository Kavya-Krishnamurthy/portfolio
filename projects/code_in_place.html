Returning to Python with Stanford's Code in Place

How a free global programming course helped me refresh my coding skills, return to research after maternity leave, and build Match-A-Genome.

When people talk about learning to code, they usually imagine a neat desk setup, a hot cup of coffee, noise-canceling headphones, and uninterrupted focus.

My version looked a little different.

Picture this: a six-month-old baby, a suitcase permanently half-packed, live classes twice a week, assignments due every week, family weddings, long-distance travel, and a traditional naming ceremony that involved shaving my baby's head and offering the hair as part of a religious ritual.

And somehow, somewhere in between all of that, I decided it was the perfect time to take Stanford University's Code in Place.

Questionable timing? Maybe.
Worth it? Absolutely.

Why I Signed Up

After taking a break for maternity leave, I knew I wanted to ease my way back into research and academia. One thing I've learned over the years is that skills have a sneaky way of disappearing when they aren't used.

Coding, especially, is one of those things.

You spend months learning concepts, solving problems, and building confidence. Then life happens. Before you know it, you're staring at a simple loop wondering if you've ever seen Python before.

I wasn't about to let that happen.

I wanted a structured way to revisit the fundamentals, rebuild confidence, and get my brain back into problem-solving mode. That's what led me to Stanford's Code in Place.

What I didn't realize was that the real challenge wasn't going to be Python.

It was going to be everything else.

Coding Between Flights, Ceremonies, and Naptime

A few weeks into the course, we had to travel to my hometown for a family wedding. Then came my baby's naming ceremony. Then came the ritual. Then came more travel.

Meanwhile, Stanford politely continued operating on schedule.

Live classes every week.
Assignments every week.
Deadlines every week.

There was no special "new mom currently living out of a suitcase" extension policy.

So I attended classes whenever I could, squeezed coding sessions into naptime, and became surprisingly efficient at debugging while simultaneously keeping a tiny human entertained.

Thankfully, I had two major advantages:

  • My study buddy — a six-month-old who supervised all coding activities with great seriousness.
  • My superhero husband — who made sure I had the time and support needed to keep going.

Together, we somehow survived every assignment, every class, and every deadline. And yes, we made it to graduation.

Why Code in Place Is So Special

If you've ever wanted to learn Python, or if you're someone who already knows Python but feels a little rusty, I genuinely cannot recommend this course enough.

Code in Place is a free global initiative created by Stanford University that brings Stanford's introductory programming curriculum to learners around the world.

And when I say around the world, I mean around the world.

Every year, thousands of students participate, learning from the same teaching philosophy and materials that have helped make Stanford's introductory programming courses so well known.

The course is beautifully designed.

The Karel programming exercises, which introduce computational thinking before diving into full Python programming, are deceptively simple and surprisingly effective. Instead of overwhelming learners with syntax, the course focuses on developing problem-solving skills first.

Every concept is immediately reinforced through coding challenges, helping build confidence while strengthening foundational programming skills.

Another thing that stood out was the volunteer mentor network.

The sheer number of volunteer section leaders who dedicate their time to helping students is remarkable. The mentor sessions created a supportive learning environment that made a large global course feel surprisingly personal.

Over six to eight weeks, the course helped me rebuild momentum, sharpen my coding fundamentals, and transition back into research mode far more smoothly than I expected.

My Final Project: Match-A-Genome

As part of the course, every student builds a final project. Given my background and interests, it probably won't surprise anyone that I wandered straight into bioinformatics.

The result was Match-A-Genome, a beginner-friendly educational game designed to introduce core bioinformatics concepts through interactive learning.

What Is Multiple Sequence Alignment?

The game focuses on Multiple Sequence Alignment (MSA), a fundamental technique used in genomics and computational biology.

Researchers use MSA to compare DNA, RNA, or protein sequences by inserting gaps so that similar regions align with one another.

Professional alignment tools use sophisticated algorithms and scoring systems. Match-A-Genome takes a more approachable route.

The goal was not to replicate research-grade software but to create a visual, interactive experience that helps beginners understand the basic idea behind sequence alignment.

How the Game Works

  • 🧬 Choose between DNA, RNA, or protein sequences
  • 🧬 Random sequences are generated for every play session
  • 🧬 Insert gaps to align matching letters vertically
  • 🧬 Correctly aligned positions are highlighted
  • 🧬 Every successful match earns one point

The scoring system provides immediate feedback, making the experience both educational and engaging.

Final Thoughts

What started as a simple goal to brush up on Python became something much more meaningful.

It reminded me that learning doesn't have to pause because life gets busy.

Sometimes learning happens during naps, airport layovers, wedding preparations, and at strange hours of the night when everyone else is asleep.

Sometimes progress looks less like a perfectly planned study schedule and more like stubbornly showing up anyway.

And honestly?

That's still progress.

A huge thank you to the Stanford professors, organizers, mentors, volunteers, my husband, and my tiny study buddy for helping make this journey possible.

Key Takeaway:
Sometimes progress doesn't happen under perfect conditions. Sometimes it happens between naps, airport layovers, family events, and a surprising amount of determination.
