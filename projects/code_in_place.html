Returning to Python with Stanford's Code in Place

How a free global programming course helped me refresh my coding skills, return to research after maternity leave, and build Match-A-Genome.

When people talk about learning to code, they usually picture a neat desk, a hot cup of coffee, noise-canceling headphones, and uninterrupted focus.

My version looked a little different.

Picture this: a six-month-old baby, a suitcase that was never fully unpacked, live classes twice a week, weekly assignments, family weddings, long-distance travel, and a traditional naming ceremony that involved shaving my baby's head and offering the hair as part of a religious ritual.

And somewhere in the middle of all that, I decided it was the perfect time to take Stanford University's Code in Place.

Questionable timing? Perhaps. Worth it? Absolutely.

Why I Signed Up

After maternity leave, I knew I wanted to ease my way back into research and academia. One thing I've learned over the years is that skills have a sneaky habit of disappearing when they aren't used.

Coding, especially, is one of those things.

You spend months learning concepts, solving problems, and building confidence. Then life happens. Before you know it, you're staring at a simple loop wondering whether you've forgotten Python or Python has forgotten you.

I wasn't about to let that happen.

I wanted a structured way to revisit the fundamentals, sharpen my problem-solving skills, and remind myself that I was still capable of writing code that did something other than produce error messages.

That's what led me to Stanford's Code in Place.

What I didn't realize was that the real challenge wasn't going to be Python.

It was going to be everything else.

Coding Between Flights, Ceremonies, and Naptime

A few weeks into the course, life decided to become unnecessarily ambitious.

There was a family wedding.

Then my daughter's naming ceremony.

Then more travel.

Meanwhile, Stanford continued operating with admirable consistency.

Live classes every week.
Assignments every week.
Deadlines every week.

Unfortunately, there was no special category for "currently traveling with an infant and several suitcases."

So I attended classes whenever I could, squeezed coding sessions into naptime, and became surprisingly efficient at debugging while simultaneously entertaining a tiny human.

Thankfully, I wasn't doing it alone.

  • My six-month-old daughter became my unofficial study buddy and full-time supervisor.
  • My husband stepped into superhero mode and made sure I had the time and support to keep going.

Together, we somehow survived every assignment, every class, and every deadline.

And yes, we made it to graduation.

Why Code in Place Is So Special

If you're looking to learn Python from scratch—or simply brush up on the basics— I genuinely cannot recommend this course enough.

Code in Place is a free global initiative that brings Stanford University's introductory programming curriculum to students around the world.

And when I say around the world, I mean around the world.

Every year, thousands of students participate, learning from the same teaching philosophy and materials that have made Stanford's introductory programming courses so well known.

One of my favorite aspects of the course was Karel, a teaching framework that introduces computational thinking before diving into full Python programming.

Instead of overwhelming learners with syntax on day one, the course focuses on building problem-solving skills first.

Every concept is reinforced through coding challenges, making the learning process both practical and engaging.

Another standout feature was the volunteer mentor community.

The sheer number of mentors who dedicate their time to helping students is remarkable. Their sessions transformed a massive global course into something that felt surprisingly personal.

Over six to eight weeks, the course helped me rebuild momentum, strengthen my coding fundamentals, and transition back into research mode far more smoothly than I expected.

My Final Project: Match-A-Genome

As part of the course, every student builds a final project.

Given my background, it probably won't surprise anyone that I eventually wandered into bioinformatics.

The result was Match-A-Genome, a beginner-friendly educational game designed to introduce bioinformatics concepts through interactive learning.

What Is Multiple Sequence Alignment?

The game focuses on Multiple Sequence Alignment (MSA), a fundamental technique used in genomics and computational biology.

Researchers use MSA to compare DNA, RNA, or protein sequences by inserting gaps so that similar regions align with one another.

Professional alignment software relies on sophisticated algorithms and scoring systems.

Match-A-Genome takes a more approachable route.

The goal wasn't to recreate research-grade software, but to create a simple, visual experience that helps beginners understand the basic idea behind sequence alignment.

How the Game Works

  • Choose between DNA, RNA, or protein sequences
  • Random sequences are generated for every session
  • Insert gaps to align matching letters vertically
  • Correct alignments are highlighted
  • Every successful match earns one point

The scoring system provides immediate feedback, making the experience both educational and engaging.

Built entirely in Python, Match-A-Genome combines coding, biology, and game design into a beginner-friendly STEM learning experience.

Final Thoughts

What started as a simple goal to brush up on Python became something much more meaningful.

It reminded me that learning doesn't have to pause because life gets busy.

Sometimes learning happens during naps, airport layovers, wedding preparations, and at strange hours of the night when everyone else is asleep.

Sometimes progress looks less like a perfectly planned study schedule and more like stubbornly showing up anyway.

Progress doesn't always look impressive in the moment.
Sometimes it just looks like not giving up.

A huge thank you to the Stanford professors, organizers, mentors, volunteers, my husband, and my tiny study buddy for making this journey possible.

Key Takeaway:
Perfect conditions are overrated. Sometimes the best learning happens between naps, flights, family events, and a healthy amount of determination.
